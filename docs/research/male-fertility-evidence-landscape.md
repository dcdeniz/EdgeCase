# Male fertility evidence landscape

- Status: Initial research synthesis
- Last reviewed: 2026-07-25
- Scope: Evidence and datasets relevant to a male reproductive-health readiness and semen-quality risk product

## Product thesis

Lifestyle, health, medication, exposure, food, and wearable data contain real information about male reproductive health. Some factors causally suppress spermatogenesis, some are supported by intervention evidence, and many show observational associations with semen parameters.

These inputs can measure determinants and predictors. They do not directly measure sperm concentration, total count, motility, morphology, DNA fragmentation, or probability of conception. A model may estimate those outcomes after suitable training and validation, but the estimate remains a prediction until checked against standardized semen testing and, for fertility claims, couple-level reproductive outcomes.

EdgeCase should present three separate outputs:

1. **Clinical profile**: measured semen, hormone, diagnosis, and relevant genetic results.
2. **Reproductive risk estimate**: a calibrated prediction with an uncertainty interval and declared endpoint.
3. **Modifiable readiness score**: transparent progress on evidence-supported behaviours.

A fourth value, **data confidence**, should describe the amount, recency, comparability, and quality of the input data.

## Outcomes are not interchangeable

Research described as `male fertility` may measure:

- reproductive hormones;
- semen volume;
- sperm concentration or total count;
- total or progressive motility;
- morphology;
- sperm DNA fragmentation;
- time to pregnancy;
- clinical pregnancy; or
- live birth.

An exposure can affect one endpoint without proven effects on the others. Every evidence card and model output must name its endpoint.

## Evidence landscape

The classifications below are product-oriented synthesis, not formal clinical GRADE ratings.

### Established clinical determinants and escalation factors

These should create warnings, exclusions, or clinician-directed pathways rather than ordinary point deductions:

- exogenous testosterone and anabolic-androgenic steroids;
- chemotherapy and testicular or pelvic radiotherapy;
- anti-androgens and selected gonadotoxic medicines;
- undescended testes, torsion, major testicular trauma, and testicular cancer;
- genital-tract obstruction, orchitis, epididymitis, and selected infections;
- pituitary or hypothalamic disease, hyperprolactinaemia, and clinically significant endocrine disease;
- cystic-fibrosis-related obstruction, chromosomal disorders, and Y-chromosome microdeletions;
- varicocele when clinically diagnosed; and
- severe systemic, kidney, or liver disease where fertility may be affected.

Exogenous testosterone can markedly suppress gonadotropins and reduce or stop spermatogenesis. Recovery after cessation may take months and, rarely, years. hCG, FSH, selective oestrogen-receptor modulators, aromatase inhibitors, and pulsatile GnRH are diagnosis-dependent clinical treatments, not consumer optimization ingredients.

SARMs, growth-hormone secretagogues, IGF-related compounds, and unlicensed peptide blends should be recorded, but direct human fertility evidence is insufficient to calculate a benefit or precise harm. Unknown composition is itself a safety concern.

### Supported modifiable factors

Factors with meaningful human evidence include:

- cigarette smoking and cumulative exposure;
- heavy or chronic alcohol exposure;
- obesity, central adiposity, insulin resistance, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome;
- poor sleep, sleep disorders, and circadian disruption;
- moderate physical inactivity and, at the opposite extreme, some high-load endurance or overtraining contexts;
- recurrent substantial heat exposure and recent high fever;
- healthier versus Western-style dietary patterns;
- occupational lead and selected pesticide, solvent, radiation, and air-pollution exposures; and
- severe energy restriction or metabolic disruption.

These factors differ substantially in evidence strength, reversibility, and effect size. The score must not give a sauna session the same status as exogenous testosterone or chemotherapy.

### Diet and food logging

Evidence is generally stronger for dietary patterns than isolated foods or nutrients.

Favourable associations commonly appear for Mediterranean-style patterns, dietary diversity, fruit, vegetables, fish, nuts, whole grains, fibre, and unsaturated-fat sources. Unfavourable associations commonly appear for Western dietary patterns, processed meat, sugar-sweetened drinks, refined carbohydrates, and high saturated- or trans-fat patterns.

Red meat, dairy, soy, caffeine, energy drinks, total protein, and individual protein sources have mixed or context-dependent evidence. Folate, zinc, selenium, vitamins C, D, and E matter in deficiency states, but supplement use beyond need is not reliably beneficial. Broad antioxidant formulations have not consistently improved semen parameters or DNA integrity in randomized trials.

Food photography can longitudinally estimate dietary pattern, diversity, and exposure frequency. Portion and nutrient estimates should carry uncertainty and user confirmation.

### Wearables and passive sensing

Wearables can directly measure or estimate relevant determinants:

- sleep duration and regularity;
- activity and sedentary time;
- training load and recovery;
- resting heart rate and illness-related change;
- weight and body-composition trends where connected; and
- longitudinal behavioural adherence.

They do not directly observe spermatogenesis. Device models and proprietary algorithms differ, especially for sleep stages, energy expenditure, and body composition. Store device provenance and prefer trends over spurious precision.

Human sleep studies associate poor sleep characteristics with lower semen measurements, while exercise trials and meta-analyses show that structured activity can alter reproductive markers in selected populations. This supports wearable-derived features in a risk model, but not a claim that a watch measured sperm count.

### Tobacco, alcohol, and recreational substances

Cigarette smoking has consistent adverse semen associations and strong broader health reasons for cessation. Heavy alcohol use is more consistently concerning than light intake. Cannabis findings across semen, hormones, time to pregnancy, and assisted-reproduction outcomes remain inconsistent.

Evidence for vaping, nicotine pouches, snus, cocaine, stimulants, ketamine, and mixed recreational drugs is less mature or confounded. Long-term opioids can suppress the reproductive endocrine axis and impair sexual function.

The product should model dose, frequency, duration, co-use, and recency rather than binary exposure alone.

### Heat, behaviour, and sexual function

Repeated sauna, hot-tub, and occupational heat exposures are biologically plausible and supported by limited human studies. Evidence is weaker for laptop placement, heated seats, tight clothing, insulating clothing, and prolonged sitting. These may support low-cost precautions but should carry small score weights.

Cycling combines training load, pressure, sitting, and possible heat; ordinary cycling should not be labelled infertile behaviour.

Libido, morning erections, erectile function, ejaculation difficulty, and sexual frequency can affect real-world fecundability and may reveal hormonal, vascular, psychological, or medication-related problems. They do not directly measure semen quality. Prefer validated questionnaires and broad trends over timing every erection.

### Plastics and endocrine-disrupting chemicals

Microplastics have been detected in human semen and reproductive tissues. Recent human studies associate seminal microplastics and plastic-tableware use with poorer sperm-quality measurements, but studies remain small, source attribution is difficult, and intervention evidence is absent.

The app may log bottled water, plastic-lined paper cups, takeaway packaging, heated plastic, plastic food containers, and chopping-board material. These data can support an emerging exposure-reduction indicator. They cannot currently support a conversion such as `one fewer plastic cup = a known sperm-count increase`.

BPA, BPS, phthalates, PFAS, PCBs, and flame retardants have substantial mechanistic and biomonitoring literature, but results differ by compound, mixture, exposure window, and endpoint. Where possible, measured biomarkers are stronger than product-use questionnaires.

### Environmental and occupational exposures

Lead has comparatively consistent human evidence. Cadmium is also concerning; evidence for mercury and arsenic depends on chemical form, dose, and study population. Pesticides, herbicides, solvents, paints, petrochemicals, dust, fumes, and ionising radiation must be captured with job, task, frequency, ventilation, protective equipment, compound, and biomonitoring details.

Location-based air-pollution estimates can enrich risk models, but remain population-level exposure proxies.

### Medicines

Medication effects vary by drug, dose, duration, indication, and baseline health. Stronger concerns exist for testosterone, anabolic steroids, sulfasalazine, anti-androgens, selected cancer therapies, selected immunosuppressants, and long-term opioids. Evidence for finasteride, dutasteride, spironolactone, ketoconazole, SSRIs, and many other psychiatric medicines is more conditional or mixed.

The product must never advise stopping prescribed treatment. It should create an evidence card and a clinician-discussion prompt.

### Nonmodifiable and developmental factors

Age, puberty, premature birth, prenatal exposures, childhood undescended testes, family history, genetic variants, and previous fertility history belong in baseline risk and clinical interpretation. They must not reduce a behaviour-readiness score.

### Measurement modifiers

The following can materially change a semen result or its comparability without representing long-term reproductive health:

- abstinence duration and ejaculation frequency;
- incomplete sample collection;
- lubricant use;
- time from ejaculation to analysis;
- transport temperature;
- home versus clinic collection;
- laboratory, technician, and analytical method;
- recent fever or acute illness;
- season; and
- possibly time of day.

EdgeCase should normalize or flag comparisons when collection conditions differ. This may prevent more false insight than many lifestyle features.

## Prediction research

A retrospective single-centre study of 5,109 men used XGBoost with modifiable lifestyle and general characteristics to predict semen-quality categories. It demonstrates predictive signal but requires external, geographic, and prospective validation.

Machine-learning studies based on the small VISEM dataset combine semen microscopy with participant information, hormones, and serum fatty acids. They are useful demonstrations but not sufficient to validate a population risk score.

The product claim supported by current research is:

> Lifestyle, health, and exposure data can predict some variation in semen quality at population level. EdgeCase uses those signals to estimate risk, prioritize action, and recommend appropriate testing; it does not replace semen analysis.

## Datasets

### VISEM

Public multimodal data from 85 adult participants, including semen microscopy video, semen analysis, sex hormones, serum fatty acids, age, BMI, and abstinence time.

- Dataset: <https://datasets.simula.no/visem/>
- Best use: computer vision and multimodal pipeline prototypes
- Limitation: far too small for a general lifestyle-to-semen clinical model

### VISEM-Tracking

Twenty videos with 29,196 annotated frames, sperm bounding boxes, semen endpoints, hormones, morphology, motility, and DNA-fragmentation information.

- Dataset: <https://datasets.simula.no/visem-tracking/>
- Paper: <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10167330/>
- Best use: sperm detection and tracking

### Longitudinal Investigation of Fertility and the Environment

The LIFE Study is a prospective preconception cohort connecting semen and sperm biomarkers, environmental exposures, lifestyle variables, time to pregnancy, and couple-level reproductive outcomes. Access is controlled through dbGaP.

- Study: <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/projects/gap/cgi-bin/study.cgi?study_id=phs001692.v1.p1>
- Best use: exposure and fecundity modelling

### N-SEED

The Nippon Semen and Environmental Exposure Database protocol integrates sperm microscopy, DNA fragmentation, environmental chemicals, endocrine profiles, lifestyle, sleep, oxidative stress, and erectile function. It is an important design reference but not yet a mature public training dataset.

- Protocol: <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13135167/>

### UCI Fertility data

The commonly reused UCI-style fertility dataset has roughly 100 observations and coarse lifestyle variables. It is appropriate for tutorials only, not a medical score, due to sample size, imbalance, weak external validity, and overfitting risk.

### Auxiliary sources

NHANES, UK Biobank, the National Survey of Family Growth, public air-pollution and temperature data, occupational exposure matrices, food-composition databases, and wearable-validation datasets can support feature engineering. They do not contain the same participants as semen datasets and must not be naively joined as individual-level training data.

## Validation requirements

A credible prospective programme should:

1. recruit a diverse cohort across multiple clinics and geographic regions;
2. obtain at least two standardized baseline semen analyses where feasible;
3. record abstinence, completeness, transport, laboratory, and analysis method;
4. collect approximately three months of wearable, diet, medication, illness, and exposure data;
5. use biomonitoring rather than questionnaire-only exposure estimates where practical;
6. predict continuous named endpoints rather than a binary `fertile` label;
7. hold out entire clinics and regions for external validation;
8. report calibration, uncertainty, missingness, and subgroup performance;
9. pre-register predictors and analysis;
10. run silent prospective validation before exposing predictions to users;
11. test whether score-guided coaching changes behaviour; and
12. separately test semen endpoints, time to pregnancy, and live birth.

Primary methodological risks are confounding, reverse causality, clinic-selection bias, data leakage, multiple testing, correlated chemical mixtures, device-specific errors, missing-not-at-random data, and regression to the mean between semen samples.

## Product rules

- Never display `fertility percentage` from lifestyle data alone.
- Never equate readiness-score change with semen improvement.
- Never turn WHO reference distributions into a fertile/infertile diagnosis.
- Never penalize genetic, congenital, cancer, or other nonmodifiable conditions as behaviour.
- Never advise starting or stopping medicine without a clinician.
- Show the source, endpoint, population, study design, evidence confidence, and last-reviewed date behind every scored factor.
- Use urgent clinical gates for testosterone, anabolic steroids, cancer therapy, severe testicular history, and other major determinants.
- Recalculate clinical change only after comparable follow-up testing.
