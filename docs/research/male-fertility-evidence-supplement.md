## Spermatogenesis timeline

The complete spermatogenic cycle takes approximately 64–74 days (Heller 1963, cited in clinical literature; Amann 2008), with epididymal transit adding 10–14 days, making the full window roughly 10–12 weeks. Individual variation spans 42–76 days. This timeline is the mechanistic basis for the "~3-month improvement window" used throughout the product.

**Verification note:** Pin the Heller 1963 citation to a primary journal volume before quoting a specific day count in-app.

### Sperm DNA fragmentation (DFI)

DFI is partly but not fully correlated with conventional parameters. Abnormal DFI can coexist with otherwise normal semen analysis.

| Intervention | Evidence | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Smoking cessation | Smoking → oxidative stress → elevated DFI; cessation improves antioxidant status | Indirect via oxidative stress pathway |
| Antioxidant supplementation (selenium, vitamin C/E) | May reduce DFI in deficiency states | PMC10500740; broad blends inconsistent in RCTs |
| Heat reduction | Heat-induced ROS increases DFI; cessation expected to improve | No DFI-specific RCT |
| Optimal ejaculation frequency | Frequent ejaculation → lower DFI; prolonged abstinence raises DFI | PMC11847665 |
| Folate + Zinc | DFI *increased* in treatment group at 6 months | FAZST RCT — avoid routine use |
| Varicocele repair | DFI improvement reported in observational literature | Not yet RCT-confirmed |

Key correlations (11,339-sample study; DOI 10.1080/19396368.2024.2333285):
- DFI negatively correlated with progressive motility (r ≈ −0.51) and concentration
- Each 1% decrease in normal sperm ratio → DFI +0.73
- Each additional minute of liquefaction time → DFI +0.13

Independence note (PMC12028733): membrane integrity, motility, and DFI are largely independent (ρ = 0.21). Present DFI as a distinct clinical dimension — poor motility does not reliably predict high DFI.

### Semen volume

| Intervention | Evidence | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Longer abstinence (≥4–7 days) | Increases volume; reduces motility and raises DFI | Trade-off; context-dependent |
| Alcohol reduction | Volume most consistently reduced by alcohol | Meta-analysis (PubMed 37159717) |
| Outdoor aerobic exercise | Greatest volume improvement vs other exercise types | NMA (PMC11913713) |

## Smoking — effect sizes and cessation timeline

### Harm
- Count: MD −9.72 × 10⁶/ml; motility: MD −3.48%; morphology: MD −1.37% (PubMed 27113031; 5,865 men; 2010 WHO methods)
- Effects larger in moderate/heavy smokers; pack-year index more informative than binary status

### Cessation
- Ragheb A et al. (*J Sex Med* 2024;21(Suppl 2):qdae002.205; DOI 10.1093/jsxmed/qdae002.205): N = **60** (correction — earlier described as 90). All parameters improved P < 0.001; positively correlated with time since cessation.
- 2025 systematic review (*Reproduction and Fertility*): significant improvement at 3 and 6 months; dose-dependent pack-year effect on recovery.
- Mechanistic basis for 3-month window: spermatogenic cycle 70–90 days.

## Alcohol — dose-response

Meta-analysis of 40 studies (*Heliyon* 2023; PubMed 37159717):
- Semen volume: most consistently reduced parameter
- Concentration, motility, morphology: no significant pooled association (heterogeneous findings)
- Testosterone, FSH, LH: all significantly reduced
- Antioxidant enzymes reduced; sperm DNA fragmentation not significantly affected

**Product note:** Model drinks per week and binge frequency separately. Do not present a specific drinks/week harm threshold — the pooled data does not support one. Heavy or chronic use is more consistently harmful than light intake.

## Obesity and metabolic health

### Harm
- Meta-analysis of 14 studies, 8,443 patients (PubMed 41427049; Frontiers Endocrinology 2025): significant differences in morphology, total motility, concentration, progressive motility, volume, and total count across BMI groups (all p < 0.05)
- 30-study review: 25/30 demonstrated worsening parameters with increasing BMI

### Weight loss and GLP-1
- Andersen E et al. (*Hum Reprod* 2022;37(7):1414–1422; DOI 10.1093/humrep/deac096; NCT04122716; n = 56):
  - 8-week low-calorie diet → improved concentration and count
  - Maintained at 1 year only with sustained weight loss (exercise or GLP-1)
  - **Verify "40% improvement" against full text before quoting — not confirmed in abstract**

## Sleep — effect sizes and testosterone mechanism

### Semen parameters
- 727 infertile male partners (PMC12487260; Oct 2023–Feb 2025): 75.1% poor sleep quality; poor sleepers had significantly lower concentration and progressive motility
- *Andrology* 2024 (DOI 10.1111/andr.13560): < 6 h → lower total and progressive motility vs 7.5–8.0 h; both short (< 6 h) and long (> 9 h) → lower semen volume; optimal window 7.5–8.5 h

### Sleep and testosterone
- Leproult R & Van Cauter E (*JAMA* 2011;305(21):2173; PMC4445839): n = 10 healthy young men (mean age 24); 1 week < 5 h → daytime testosterone reduced **10–15%**; lowest 2–10 pm on restricted days. This study measured testosterone, not semen parameters directly.

## Exercise — detail

### Moderate exercise benefit
NMA (14 RCTs, 1,079 subjects; PMC10258032; PMC11913713):
- Indoor aerobic, outdoor aerobic, resistance training all reduced necrotic spermatozoa and increased active spermatozoa vs inactivity
- Outdoor aerobic: greatest volume improvement
- MICT superior to HICT for sperm concentration

### Overtraining harm
*Fertil Steril* 1993 (S0015-0282(16)56223-2); n = 5 endurance-trained men:
- Doubled weekly training volume for 2 weeks → count −43% immediately, −52% at 3 months
- Testosterone fell; cortisol rose; inverse correlation r = −0.92
- Recovery: ~3 months after returning to normal training
- **Caveat:** n = 5; values remained within normal range; confirm author list before citing by name

## Mediterranean diet — effect sizes

NMA (PMC12276387; PubMed 40419219; 2025):
- Sperm count: MD +24.37 M (95% CI 1.30–47.44; I² = 89%)
- Total motility: MD +8.81% (95% CI 2.26–15.37; I² = 88%)
- Progressive motility: MD +7.49% (95% CI 1.47–13.50; I² = 86%)
- Normal morphology: MD +1.02% (95% CI 0.21–1.82; I² = 77%)
- Evidence from 2 included RCTs aligned with primary results
- High I² in observational data — disclose heterogeneity in every evidence card

## Supplements — effect sizes by agent

Estimates apply to men with idiopathic infertility or OAT — not necessarily a general population.

**L-carnitine / L-acetylcarnitine**
- PMC8898892 (23 RCTs, 1,917 patients): motility WMD +6.52% (2.55–10.05); morphology WMD +4.96% (0.20–9.73)
- Advances Nutr 2022 (DOI 10.1016/j.advnut.2021.12.006): motility MD +12.43% (4.07–20.26; SUCRA 88.7%) — ranked first
- OAT NMA (PubMed 37495550; 29 RCTs, 2,045 men): L-carnitine + LAC top-ranked

**Omega-3 fatty acids**
- PMC8898892: concentration WMD +9.89 × 10⁶/ml (7.01–12.77) — ranked first for concentration
- No significant pregnancy-rate effect vs placebo in NMAs

**CoQ10**
- Advances Nutr 2022: concentration MD +5.95 (0.05–10.79; SUCRA 79.4%); motility MD +7.33% (0.35–14.17)

**Selenium (100–300 µg/day, 3–11 months)**
- Improved concentration, total motility, morphology across multiple trials (PMC10500740)

**Zinc (66–500 mg/day, 3–6 months)**
- Concentration +~1.48 × 10⁶ spermatozoa/ml; improved total motility

**Vitamin D (~4,000 IU/day)**
- 5 trials, n = 648: ~5% total motility improvement; modest morphology gains

**Folate + Zinc — CAUTION**
- FAZST RCT (NCT01857310): no semen quality improvement; no live birth improvement; DFI significantly *higher* at 6 months in treatment arm
- Do not recommend routine use without confirmed deficiency

**Broad antioxidant formulations:** inconsistent semen and DFI improvement in RCTs; do not conflate with individual agent evidence.

## Air pollution — effect sizes and mitigation

### Harm
Environmental Science & Technology 2023 meta-analysis (PubMed 36731563):
- Sperm density: −5.69%; total count: −15.09%; progressive motility: −1.63%; total motility: −2.30%
- Stronger in older men and those with lower education

Frontiers Public Health 2023 meta-analysis (93,996 Chinese men): confirms negative direction across PM2.5, PM10, SO₂, NO₂.

PMC11905585 (2025): further confirms association.

Mechanism: PM2.5, O₃, NO₂ → increased ROS → oxidative stress → sperm damage and DFI elevation.

### HEPA purifier evidence
No RCT of HEPA use has measured semen endpoints. Frame as multi-step inference:

> "HEPA filtration reduces indoor PM2.5 by up to 52%. PM2.5 is associated with lower sperm count and motility in population studies. No trial has yet tested HEPA use against semen outcomes directly."

- HAFTRAP pragmatic crossover (N = 119; 1 month HEPA / washout / 1 month sham): **52% indoor PM2.5 reduction**
- RAPIDS Detroit intervention (PMC7021209): substantial personal PM2.5 reduction with HEPA
- Classroom HEPA crossover (PMC12234348): 42.31% personal PM2.5 reduction from living-room HEPA; 21.34% from classroom HEPA

**N95 masks:** substantially reduce PM2.5 inhalation. Cloth and surgical masks not validated for fine particulate filtration. Locate a specific personal-exposure primary study before making quantified mask recommendations.

## Heat exposure — detail

### Scrotal temperature increases
- Thighs approximated for 60 minutes (no laptop): scrotal temperature rose **+2.1°C** (sourced via laptop-study context; Human Reprod 2005;20(2):452 — confirm original identifiers before quoting)
- Conventional office chair 20 minutes: ~+3°C in some studies
- Paraplegic men in wheelchairs (fixed thigh approximation): higher deep scrotal temperature and reduced sperm motility vs able-bodied men

### Sauna and hot tubs
- Regular Finnish sauna (≥2 sessions/week, 80–90°C): reductions in motility and concentration
- 2025 *Fertility and Sterility* study: regular hot-bath exposure → substantially lower semen parameters
- Hot tub use: poorer parameters; improvement after cessation

### Recovery
- Most men: baseline recovery within 45–60 days after reducing heat exposure
- Full recovery may require up to 3–6 months (one spermatogenic cycle)
- Peak count reduction typically appears 4–5 weeks post-heat-exposure onset — **verify against primary heat study before quoting in-app**

### Preconception cohort (McKinnon 2022)
Male personal heat exposures and fecundability. *Andrology* 2022; DOI 10.1111/andr.13242. Couple-level fecundability context beyond semen parameters.

## Ejaculation frequency and abstinence protocols

### Evidence
- PMC12257329 (shorter abstinence NMA; WJMH): ART outcomes significantly better with abstinence < 4 days
- *Andrology* 2024 (Du et al.; DOI 10.1111/andr.13583): dose-response meta-analysis — ≥4–7 days increases volume and count but reduces motility and raises DFI
- PMC11847665 (23,527-analysis retrospective): frequent ejaculation → superior motility and DNA integrity

### Protocol by context

**Natural conception:**
- Ejaculation every 1–2 days during fertile window; abstinence > 4–5 days reduces motility without adding useful count

**IUI / IVF preparation:**
- > 4 days abstinence → lower fertilisation, blastocyst formation, implantation, and pregnancy rates
- Couples who conceived: mean abstinence closer to 3 days; implantation highest at 1 day
- Recommend ~2–3 days abstinence for sample collection

**Semen analysis appointment:**
- WHO recommends 2–7 days; recent evidence favours 2–3 days for results predictive of ART outcomes
- Document abstinence duration with every analysis to enable valid comparisons

## Cannabis — detail

### Harm
ASRM 2024 committee opinion: advise against cannabis use when attempting conception.

2023 systematic review:
- Concentration: average −22% in cannabis users vs never-users (verify CI against primary meta-analysis before quoting)
- Strict morphology: 33.1% abnormal (never-users) vs 50.7% (current users) vs 53.4% (past users)
- Motility, antioxidant activity, and DNA integrity: reductions documented
- Dose-dependent effects

Mechanism (PLOS One 2024; DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0298697): THC alters intracellular signalling in spermatozoa; disrupts capacitation and pre-implantation development; epigenetic modifications documented.

THC and CBD both affect CatSper calcium channels (*Hum Reprod* 2025;40(5):796): oppositional effects documented — further research required before clinical scoring.

### Caveat
Findings across time to pregnancy, ART outcomes, and hormones remain inconsistent. Record dose, frequency, form (smoked/vaped/edible), and duration rather than binary use status.

## Heavy metals — detail

### Lead
2025 systematic review and meta-analysis (PMC12695014; search to April 2024; 17 studies):
- Elevated blood lead → reduced semen volume, concentration, total count, vitality, and total motility

Earlier meta-analysis (PubMed 41370422): consistent findings.

### Cadmium
- Elevated seminal cadmium associated with increased male infertility risk
- Low-quality semen: significantly higher cadmium and barium concentrations (ScienceDirect NMA, search to Nov 2024)

### Mechanism (PMC12384910; 2025 narrative review)
Heavy metals disrupt HPG-axis hormones and spermatogenesis via endocrine disruption and oxidative stress. Bioaccumulation makes chronic low-level exposure potentially more damaging than acute exposure.

**Product note:** Capture job type, tasks, years of exposure, ventilation, PPE. Where possible, biomonitoring (blood lead level) is stronger than questionnaire-based estimates. Flag elevated occupational lead risk for clinician review.

## Microplastics — 2024/2025 evidence

### Detection in semen
- 2025 study (45 men; DOI 10.3390/toxics13070566): microplastics in ~76% of samples; average 17 particles/gram semen; 15 polymer types
- PMC11663775 (multi-site China): dose-response — each additional polymer type → lower total count, concentration, progressive motility

### Mechanisms
- Polystyrene microplastics reduce vitality and motility in a time-dependent manner in vitro; increase ROS and DFI

### Systematic reviews
- PMC11697325: digestive, reproductive, respiratory impacts
- PubMed 38287142: reproductive-outcomes systematic review
- PubMed 41761215: human in-vivo micro-/nanoplastics

### Evidence limits
Studies remain small; source attribution difficult; intervention evidence (avoiding specific plastics improves semen quality) absent. App may log exposure proxies; must not convert them into a quantified sperm-count claim.

## Vasectomy reversal

### Belker et al. — canonical reference (CONFIRMED)
Belker AM, Thomas AJ Jr, Fuchs EF, Konnak JW, Sharlip ID. "Results of 1,469 microsurgical vasectomy reversals by the Vasovasostomy Study Group." *J Urol* 1991;145(3):505–511. **PubMed 1997700.**
- 9 years, 5 institutions; 1,012 first-time procedures with post-operative analyses
- Patency: **86%** (865/1,012)
- Pregnancy: **52%** (421/810 couples)
- Significant time-dependent gradient: patency and pregnancy rates substantially worse as vasectomy-to-reversal interval increases

### Azoospermia and centrifugation
WHO 6th ed. (2021): azoospermia = no spermatozoa in centrifuged sediment. Cryptozoospermia: absent from fresh preparations, present in centrifuged pellet.

Detection rates after enhanced centrifugation:
- PMC4811238 (microcentrifugation technique): **14.2%** of azoospermic samples sperm-positive
- PMC12112935 (andrological lab technique): **23.9%** sperm-positive

**Product note:** An azoospermic result without documented centrifugation should not be treated as confirmed. Present this caveat in the vasectomy and cancer tracks.

Post-reversal monitoring cadence: confirm against current AUA or EAU guideline before presenting to users — do not source from clinic marketing pages.

## Hormone panel — detail (CONFIRMED)

"Circulating sex hormone levels in relation to male sperm quality." BMC Urology 2020. **PMC7367383.** Nanjing Jinling Hospital; 338 men in subfertile couples; 2012–2015.

Measurements: LH, FSH, total testosterone, estradiol, SHBG. Analysis: generalised linear regression, adjusted for age, BMI, smoking, alcohol.

Key findings:
- After adjustment: LH, FSH, and TT all inversely associated with sperm motility (p-trend < 0.05 each)
- Mutual adjustment: only LH retained independent inverse association with motility
- Supports including a hormone panel in the clinical profile layer; caution against simple single-hormone scoring given high mutual confounding

## Varicocele repair

### 2023 meta-analysis (CONFIRMED)
"Does Varicocele Repair Improve Conventional Semen Parameters? A Meta-Analytic Study of Before-After Data." WJMH 2023. **PubMed 37382284.**
- Significant improvements: concentration, total count, total motile count, progressive motility, total motility, morphology (all p < 0.001)
- Sperm vitality: only parameter not significant

**Product note:** Varicocele belongs in the clinical pathway layer (clinician-directed), not the readiness score. When a user reports a diagnosed varicocele, generate a clinician-discussion prompt and flag it as a potentially reversible contributor.

## Cancer and fertility preservation

### Pre-treatment baseline
- ~27% of men with cancer have abnormal semen parameters before treatment; ~7% (range 1–17%) have baseline azoospermia
- Source: *Hum Reprod Open* 2024; DOI 10.1093/hropen/hoae006
- **Implication:** Pre-treatment baseline testing justified even for lower-toxicity regimens

### Post-treatment azoospermia
- ~23% develop azoospermia after treatment (verify against *Hum Reprod Update* or *Fertil Steril* before quoting)

### Surgical sperm retrieval
- Success: 37–44% per patient; live birth: 40–59% per couple

### ASCO Guideline (current standard)
ASCO 2024 (*J Clin Oncol*; DOI 10.1200/JCO-24-02782): discuss fertility preservation with all cancer patients of reproductive age before treatment begins.

### Figures pending verification
The following statistics require resolution to primary sources before in-app use: 41%/40%/7% banking rates by cancer type; counselling doubling banking from ~6–8% to 17.6%; one programme 3.3% → 19.3%; specialist contact ~30× more likely to bank; 35% cumulative live-birth rate; ~44% at-risk men actually banking.

## Semen measurement modifiers — additional data

### Abstinence and variability
- Within-person variability between ejaculates is often as large as between-person variability
- Minimum two standardised analyses (matched abstinence, lab, method) needed for stable baseline
- EdgeCase should only allow valid comparisons when collection conditions are documented and comparable
